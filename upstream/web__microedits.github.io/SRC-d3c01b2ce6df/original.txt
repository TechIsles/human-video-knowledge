Abstract

CCS Concepts

In film editing, every frame matters. While macro, content-level
edits shape the high-level narrative of a film, small local edits often
control timing and direct visual attention. For example, editors can
adjust the precise timing of clips to control the length of a meaningful silence, or cut to shots that highlight a specific character’s
reactions to important moments. These small, detailed edits are
slow and tedious to make in standard editing tools because users
have to map high-level narrative goals to low-level frame-by-frame
adjustments. At the same time, these fine-scale adjustments, which
we call micro-edits, can be nuanced and expressive, with different
choices serving different creative goals. In this work, we start by
conducting a formative study with professional editors to understand the structure of micro-edits and how they are used. We then
derive a mathematical formalization for this structure to develop
tools that leverage AI and optimization to make exploring microedits simpler and more efficient for users. Finally, we validate our
tools in multiple user studies and a wide range of video examples.

Introduction

Video editing happens at multiple temporal scales, and editors tend
to work in a coarse-to-fine manner. At the coarse scale, they begin
with what we call macro-edits, which determine the selection and
ordering of video clips. Macro-edits establish a basic timeline to create what is commonly referred to as a rough cut [32]. While novice
filmmakers and AI-based editing tools often limit editing to rough
cuts, skilled filmmakers typically make additional fine-scale edits
by decoupling audio and visual transitions and shifting them along
a given timeline to control pacing and direct audience attention.
These fine-scale edits, which we call micro-edits, offer significant
expressive control over a scene’s narrative [8, 28]. However, they
require expertise to execute well and remain tedious even for skilled

editors [14]. In this work, we address these challenges by developing new interactive tools to make micro-edits easier to explore and
apply, focusing on dialogue scenes, which are especially common
in narrative film [11]. Our work comprises four parts:
• First, we examine how and why expert filmmakers use microedits through a formative user study, detailed in Section 3. We
found that experts use micro-edits in two distinct ways: to manipulate the timing of a narrative (rhythm edits), and to direct
visual attention (view edits).
• Next, we formalize the space of possible micro-edits for a given
scene in Section 4, and use our formulation to describe different editing constraints and objectives. This allows us to address
micro-editing as an optimization problem and incorporate analyses provided by vision-language models (VLMs), when available.
• Then, building on our formalization, we design novel interactive
tools that leverage optimization and AI to efficiently explore
micro-edits through interactions that map more clearly and consistently to common creative goals.
• Finally, we validate our tools in Section 6 through a crowdsourced
study and two evaluations with 16 experienced editors, showing
that they reduce mechanical effort and support broader exploration of micro-edits.
Our work combines user-centered design with the development
of novel technical tools. The design contributions in our work deal
with understanding how micro-edits are used so we can build better
editing tools, while our technical contributions address challenges
that emerge when applying computation to that understanding.
Our formative findings translate into two design goals: efficient
exploration and useful suggestions (Section 3.5). We use these goals
to motivate and evaluate our tools.

2

Related Work

While our work is, to our knowledge, the first to address interactive
tools for micro-editing, prior work in vision, graphics, and HCI has
looked at related problems, including rough cut assembly.

2.1

Macro-Level Editing and Assembly

Computational tools for video editing primarily target content selection and rough-cut assembly. Systems for scripted dialogue encode
cinematographic idioms to decide which clips appear and in what
order [20, 21]. Transcript-based interfaces [7, 36, 51] enable textdriven manipulation, while keyword analysis recommends B-roll
insertion points [18]. More recent LLM-powered systems infer editorial intent from natural language [54], combine language with
sketch-based control [50], and support human–AI co-creation for
generating rough-cuts [19, 42]. Recent work on automated montage assembly learns shot selection, trimming, and cut placement
from professionally edited films, but does not provide explicit editor control over micro-edits, including decoupled audio and video
transitions [41]. These systems operate at word- or shot-level granularity, which is effective for structuring a scene, but they stop short
of the frame-level refinement where professional editors shape
timing and attention.

2.2

Automated Cut Placement and Pacing

Commercial tools provide filler-word removal and silence compression to streamline rough cuts [6, 12, 31]. Adobe Premiere plugins
like AutoCut [2025] and FireCut [2025] extend this by offering configurable offsets within silence-removal workflows, where audio is
cut earlier than video by a fixed amount to smooth transitions (split
edits). These workflows treat pacing as uniform temporal compression and treat split edits as mechanical timing offsets. Such behavior
is well-suited to high-volume social and podcast workflows with
strict duration constraints and tolerance for jump-cut rhythm, but
misaligned with dramatic editing where silence is expressive and
overlap is applied selectively to anticipate or extend specific beats.
Our work formalizes these micro-editing operations and introduces
an interactive tool for selective, frame-level refinement of rhythm
and visual emphasis after rough-cut assembly. Rather than applying
uniform pacing or fixed offsets, our system surfaces alternatives
and perceptual cues tied to narrative beats and viewer attention.
The closest prior work at comparable granularity is Berthouzoz
et al. [4], who analyze audio-visual features in single-speaker interview footage to locate frames where the subject is quiet and
still, enabling hidden transitions via graph-based frame selection.
Our work differs in three respects: (1) we address multi-clip dialogue with multiple speakers and camera angles; (2) we support
audio–video decoupling through split edits rather than solely audioaligned straight cuts; and (3) we provide interactive exploration of
alternatives rather than optimizing for a single global solution.

2.3

Micro-Temporal Signals for Editing

Editors time cuts to perceptual events that guide attention and
maintain continuity [46, 47]. Conventions such as cutting midaction [14], decoupling picture and sound cut points, and avoiding
cuts on blinks exploit motion continuity, dialogue structure, and
audio-visual synchrony [28, 40]. Galvane et al. [17] encode some
continuity constraints over screen position, gaze, and motion, and
optimize shot sequences accordingly in virtual environments where
scene state is available. In practice, these constraints often compete,
and their relative importance depends on narrative context.
In real footage, these cues can be inferred from audio-visual
signals, including gaze and blink via facial landmark tracking [23]
and audio-visual synchrony [33, 49]. Prior work aligns or matches
video using appearance similarity [55], motion trajectories or optical flow for action synchronization [1, 3, 9], and learned features
for continuity scoring and transition classification [34, 35].
Semantics can further indicate which alignments are editorially salient. Recent VLMs, when supplied with numbered-frame
inputs [56] and short, densely sampled clips [37], can identify such
moments (e.g., a door slam, a character’s turn). We build on these
semantic and audio-visual cues to surface micro-edit opportunities
within an interactive workflow.

Background and Formative Study

We conducted a formative study with six experienced editors (5–15
years experience) to understand how and why they adjust rough
cuts. Full details of our study are included as supplemental material,
and we summarize our findings below.

3.1

Background

Our study design incorporates additional background related to
how editors often discuss cuts, which represent transitions on a
timeline (video, audio, or both) from one piece of source media to
another. Note that the term “cut” has two meanings in editing: it can
refer to a transition point between clips, or to a version of the edit
(as in “rough cut” or “final cut”). Here we discuss cuts as transition
points. Editors classify most cuts into one of three categories:
• Straight cuts happen when video and audio transition together.
• L-cuts happen when video transitions before audio.
• J-cuts happen when audio transitions before video.
Additionally, the term “split cut” is sometimes used to describe
any cut that is not a straight cut. Implicit in these categories is an
assumption that both audio and video transition between corresponding source media. While not always true, this describes most
cuts found outside specific genres like music videos.
Macro-editing is often limited to straight cuts, and previous
computational tools have made this an explicit assumption when
formulating rough cut assembly [19, 20, 51]. This points to L- and
J-cuts as offering some insight into how editors think about microediting, which we leverage in our formative study design.

3.2

Study Design

Each participant was given rough cuts of two scripted scenes (each
approximately one-minute long) and tasked with editing each scene
in an NLE of their choice under four conditions. In one condition,
participants were unrestricted. For each of the remaining three
conditions, we asked participants to limit edits to different cut
types: straight cuts, L-cuts, or J-cuts. Participants were recorded
and asked to explain their reasoning aloud while editing, and after
completing their tasks, each participant filled out a survey.

3.3

Formative Findings

F1) Every Frame Matters: Our study reinforced the common claim
that skilled editors often make decisions at the scale of individual
frames [14, 27]. While making micro-edits, participants described
“going frame by frame, removing one frame here, adding a few there”
(P5) or “adding 3 or 4 more frames” (P1) to get transitions to feel
just right. This process also involved repeatedly re-watching parts
of video to compare the feel of different choices.
F2) Contextual Information is Important: Editing decisions
often depend on local context. For example, editors generally place
audio cuts in silent parts of a clip. On the other hand, they often
adjust visual cuts based on narrative goals (e.g., cutting to see a
particular character’s reaction during a dramatic moment), or following the “cut on action” principle [8, 14], where visuals before and
after a cut should show the continuity of some visible action (e.g.,
a gesture, blink, or gaze turn). This type of higher-level reasoning
helps editors narrow their choice of edit.
F3) Control Over Rhythm & Pacing: Editors often start with
edits meant to control the precise rhythm or pacing of a scene—
usually by inserting or removing time at the ends of adjacent clips.
We call this type of adjustment a rhythm edit. Rhythm edits change

how events are distributed on the timeline; for example, in dialogueheavy scenes, they can be used to control the length of pauses in
conversation. Half of our participants described focusing exclusively on the timing of dialogue while making such edits, with one
(P6) describing this process as starting with a “radio edit”, and another (P4) saying “I’m going to close my eyes and listen ... this lets me
really focus on the flow and not be interrupted by some video breaks.”
Rhythm edits require shifting a clip along the output timeline, often
in addition to adjusting the duration of a clip.
F4) Control Over Visual Attention: Later edits often focus on
controlling visual attention. We can think of these edits, which
we call view edits, as controlling the viewpoint of the audience
without changing the timeline of events that are happening. View
edits are where participants tended to make L-cuts and J-cuts. More
specifically, L-cuts were mostly used to make visual transitions
happen sooner, while J-cuts were used to make them happen later.
In both cases, this was usually done without changing the audio
track. To quote one participant (P1), “I already changed the timing
of the audio... so I don’t wanna, like, mess with that.”
F5) Inserting New Cuts For Reactions & Cutaways: In our study,
most edits involved adjusting existing cuts. The main exception to
this happened when editors wanted to add a reaction or cutaway
shot, where visuals transition away from the speaker or current
source of audio. Two reasons were given for such edits: first, to
direct visual attention; and second, to mask jump cuts (created when
trimming pauses within dialogue). Also, editors often repurpose
visuals from other parts of a scene to create reaction shots. This
points to conditions where reordering clips in a scene is acceptable.

3.4

Design Insights

F1 and F2 reflect that micro-editing does not have a unique solution,
and precise user control is important, though evaluating contextual
and semantic information may help automate some decisions.
F3 and F4 suggest that editors treat rhythm and views separately;
thus, controls for these aspects should be independent. This separation reflects how editors exploit audio-visual synchrony, as one
participant (P1) articulated: “What you hear and what you see are two
different systems... if you introduce the hearing as one sense and then
introduce the sight, it’s a different feeling than if you introduce both
at the same time.” Our tools are designed to help users focus on each
type of control separately. F5 suggests that showing only speakers
means audiences miss half of the story—character reactions—so
editors spend significant time scrubbing through takes to find them.
A tool that surfaces candidates would help.

3.5

Different micro-edits may accomplish different creative goals, which
can be highly context-dependent. This lack of a single correct solution complicates automation, but there is still a great deal that
optimization and AI can offer to improve existing workflows. Microedits are not random, and the space of all possible edits is much
larger than the set that meet likely creative goals. With this in mind,
we focus on two overarching design goals in this work:

4

Problem Formulation

We describe editing as a two-stage process, starting with macroedits, followed by micro-edits. We use this framing to distinguish
two types of editing decisions, not to claim that editing proceeds
strictly in this order in practice. The input to this process is a set of
source media and optional narrative description. Source media is
given as a set M = {𝑀 ∅, 𝑀 1, ...} of clips, where 𝑀 ∅ represents the
empty clip (i.e., what is displayed if part of a timeline is left empty).
The narrative, if provided (e.g., in the form of a script or storyboard),
is represented with a sequence of narrative elements S = [𝑠 1, 𝑠 2, ...],
where each 𝑠𝑖 describes a different part of the narrative. We use 𝑠𝑖 ⊂
𝑀 𝑗 to indicate that 𝑠𝑖 is portrayed in source media 𝑀 𝑗 , and 𝑀 𝑗 (𝑠𝑖 )
to denote the part of 𝑀 𝑗 that depicts 𝑠𝑖 . We leave the definition of
narrative elements [𝑠 1, 𝑠 2, ...] intentionally loose, as their primary
role is to segment source media and express partial orderings during
macro-editing. We will introduce more precise notation later for
discussing narrative events in micro-edits.

4.1

Rough Cuts & Macro-Editing

The goal of macro-editing is to produce a rough cut R, given as an
ordered sequence of source clips. When a narrative is provided, R
is determined by a mapping r from each 𝑠𝑖 ∈ S to a corresponding

What makes this “rough” is twofold: clip boundaries are left inexact, and cuts are restricted to straight cuts. From the editor’s
perspective, micro-editing refines the former and relaxes the latter.
Mathematically, a rough cut makes the set of potential clips discrete and finite. In this case, as r maps from one discrete finite set
to another, the space of possible rough cuts becomes discrete and
finite. We can represent each R in this space as a path through the
directed graph of possible clips, where edges encode the ordering
constraints given by narrative S. If S is not provided, this graph
is fully connected, and we need to limit the maximum number of
clips to keep the space of rough cuts finite.

4.3

Micro-Editing Constraints & Context

We identify two types of constraints that impact micro-editing:
• Synchrony constraints ensure that visible sound sources are heard
(e.g., we hear a character who is visibly speaking). This constraint
is one-directional: hearing off-screen sound is acceptable, which
enables L- and J-cuts. We quantify this via synchrony [13, 39, 52]:
audio and video should stay coupled in high-synchrony regions.

Micro-Edits: Computational Support for Fine-Scale Edits in Film

Unlike rhythm and view edits, which adjust existing cuts, reaction
shots add new visual material on an overlay track (B-roll) without
altering the primary audio timeline (F5). We identify candidate
segments where 𝜎 (𝑀 𝑗 , 𝑡) = 0 (subject visible but not speaking), the
target subject is detected in at least 80% of frames, and the segment
is adjacent to spoken material. To support browsing, candidates are
indexed by subject identity and emotion using EmoCLIP [16]. The
interface groups results by character and emotion (Figure 3A). This
supports both expressive goals (showing a character’s reaction) and
practical needs (masking jump cuts from rhythm tightening).

5.5

Implementation

We implemented MicroEditor as a web-based editor forked from
ClipJS [25], extending it with multi-track timeline support, rolling
edits, and the tools described above. An offline pipeline preprocesses
each source clip to extract word-level timestamps (via ASR [38]),
per-frame synchrony scores, and subject identity. For synchrony,
we use active speaker detection [22] for dialogue scenes and an
audio-visual alignment network [26] for non-dialogue content. We
threshold the continuous signal to obtain 𝜎 (𝑀 𝑗 , 𝑡) ∈ {0, 1}, where
𝜎 = 1 indicates salient audio-visual alignment (e.g., an on-screen
face visibly speaking in dialogue scenes) and 𝜎 = 0 otherwise
(Figure 5); see Appendix B for VLM prompts and more details. All
VLM queries use Gemini 3.0 Flash. VLMs are used to annotate source
clips during preprocessing (e.g., subject identity and shot type) and
to search for cut-on-action candidates, where our formalization
bounds the search to the editing window 𝑊 v (Section 5.3).

Evaluation

We first validated our synchrony analysis through a crowdsourced
study, and then evaluated MicroEditor in two comparative studies
with experienced editors, focusing on our design goals: whether
our tools enable more efficient exploration of the micro-edit space
(DG1), and whether our defaults and suggestions help editors identify desirable edits (DG2). Study 1 (N = 6) evaluated the initial system and validated tools for rhythm and view editing. Based on feedback, we refined the interface and added a Reaction Shot Browser.
Study 2 (N = 10) evaluated this expanded system with an additional
focus on reaction shot selection and perceived improvements in
scene pacing and visual flow. Because micro-editing has no single
correct solution and desirable edits depend on creative goals (Section 3.5), our evaluation emphasizes workflow support, decision
support, and exploration rather than correctness against a fixed
ground truth. Full study protocols and other evaluation details can
be found in our supplemental materials.

Synchrony Evaluation

To validate our method for decoupling audio and video, we conducted a Prolific study (𝑁 = 47; 4 excluded for failing attention
checks). Participants viewed 12 randomized clips (∼5 s each) across
three conditions: Optimized (cut inside 𝑊 ), Boundary (cut on the
window edge), and Out-of-Window (fixed offset outside the window). For each clip, participants reported whether they noticed any
distracting lip-sync errors or AV mismatches and rated how natural
or smooth the cut felt on a 7-point scale (1 = very jarring, 7 = very

smooth). Results show a significant main effect of condition on
naturalness (𝑝 < .001, Friedman test). Post-hoc tests confirmed
Optimized cuts were rated significantly more natural than both
Boundary (𝑝 = .001) and Out-of-Window (𝑝 < .001) conditions.
In aggregate, Optimized clips achieved the lowest violation rate
(32.4%) and highest naturalness (𝑀 = 5.27), vs. Out-of-Window
(85.1%, 𝑀 = 3.10) (Figure 7). Notably, excluding the Soup Spoon
scene where upstream face-tracking errors produced an unreliable
synchrony window, the Optimized violation rate drops to 19.9%
(𝑀 = 5.67). We note that our method’s performance is inherently
bounded by the capabilities of the underlying AV-sync model.

6.2

User Studies

We recruited 6 professional video editors for Study 1 (3 female, 3
male, aged 18–27) with 5–15 years of experience, all primarily using
Adobe Premiere Pro, and 10 video editors for Study 2 (6 female, 4
male, aged 19–28, 1–10 years of experience) working across narrative films, commercial content, documentaries, and YouTube. Both
studies followed the same protocol: each participant edited two
one-minute dialogue scenes (“Pumpkin Princess” and “Anesthesia”)
under a manual condition (all features disabled) and an assisted
condition (all features enabled), in counterbalanced order. Sessions
lasted 60 minutes. Participants rated feature usefulness and creativity support on 7-point Likert scales (Table 1). Study 2 additionally
collected ratings on pacing and visual flow, and logged all interactions with millisecond timestamps; one participant’s (P9) log was

Rhythm Editing: In Study 1, editors found the pre-roll and trim/
extend hotkeys useful (median 6.0/7) and efficient (median 7.0/7;
DG1). Pre-roll enabled the repeated listening behavior essential for
rhythm editing (F3). P3 emphasized repetition: “As an editor I like
to hear and see the cut at least 10 times and it was easy to replay it
using the pre-roll key.” P6 articulated why context matters: “I think
the R [pre-roll] feature is something that I really like to just quickly
go back... you kind of have to get a little bit of context further, just to
get into the flow of that, and then you can watch the cut again.”
Character Focus: Participants valued the automated L/J cuts as a
starting point that offloaded mechanical work for creative decisions.
P3 appreciated the cognitive offloading: “You always know in a
scene which character is the main one... it was nice to get [character
focus] done straight away so I can focus on other things.” P2 said: “I
used one pass to emphasize speaker and was blown away with how
accurately it created the space for the character.” P1 confirmed: “There
weren’t many specific edits I needed to keep making based off of those
automatic cuts, and those cuts would have taken a while manually.”
Character Focus was rated 7.0/7 (median), validating F4 and DG2.
In Study 2, P3 similarly noted that the automated cuts “tended to
feel natural and would be similar to what I would do as an editor
without these tools.”
Cut-on-Action: Cut-on-Action markers (median 7.0/7) transformed
the tedious frame-level search into discrete choices (F2). P5 noted:
“Cut on Action suggestions took the painstaking process of having to
find each moment of large action and match it with another clip and
made it extremely simple, with very strong accuracy.” P1 described
the reduction in decision fatigue: “Just having the markers to choose
from helped prevent decision fatigue. When you have so many frames
versus just the three options, it helps you narrow it down.” In Study 2,
P4 described using markers for narrative construction: “I used this
feature to help inform my overall approach to the video narrative
for each scene. If there was a particular narrative goal I wanted to
achieve, I used the Cut-on-Action as a framework to build it.” P3
noted how unexpected suggestions expanded creative thinking:
“Some of the options were a bit out of the blue even, as those made
me realize the amount of flexibility I had in editing, when I would
only usually consider an L/J cut difference of only a second.” Editing
is inherently creative, and there is no fixed ground truth. Still, we
observed that when cut-on-action is a strong cue, timelines often
show close alignment between manual and MicroEditor workflows.
For example, the final cut in the Anesthesia scene resulted in nearly
identical offsets across both methods. Appendix A provides a visual comparison of these timelines alongside the system’s internal
synchrony analysis.
Blink Markers: Blink markers were rated 5.5/7 in Study 1 and 4.5/7
in Study 2 (medians); they were the only feature to receive a rating
below 3 (one editor), and no other feature was rated below 4, as only
4 of 16 editors across both studies reported using cut-before-blink
in their regular workflow. P3 explained when they matter: “When

everything is technically right, but it doesn’t feel like the cut is right...
I use the blink.” P5 found them useful for pacing: “Using [blink] I
was able to keep track of the rhythm of blinking... very helpful in
terms of pacing.” Across the remaining tools, Study 2 ratings were
consistent with Study 1, confirming these findings with a larger
and more diverse participant pool (Table 1).
Exploration and Iteration: Participants rated exploration highly
(median 6.0/7) and agreed the system encouraged trying different
cuts (median 6.5/7). P5 noted that suggestions led to discoveries:
“Suggestions came up; I began to think about different edits and ended
up choosing several of them.” P2 added: “Seeing those options does
help... Otherwise I would be too rooted in my own interpretation.”
Participants also requested better support for finding and inserting reaction shots, which required tedious scrubbing through
footage (F5). Based on this feedback, we added a Reaction Shot
Browser for Study 2.
Reaction Shots: The Reaction Shot Browser was the highest-rated
feature (median 7.0/7; 9/10 rated 7/7; DG2). Editors described it as
“Emotionally satisfying” (P7), perspective-expanding (P4: “I never
really used reaction shots as often as I could have, however, this system
opened up a perspective”), and helpful for “break[ing] the homogeneity of long dialogues” (P5). Editors used reactions both for masking
jump cuts from rhythm tightening (P3, P4, P8, P9) and for directing
attention toward the listener’s emotional response. P7 discovered
unexpected narrative possibilities: “Some of the reaction shots created
new meanings (for example, one character’s body language slumping
in a chair) which influenced the choice of what shot to end the scene
with” (DG1). The system supports two complementary insertion
methods: highlighting a phrase in the interactive transcript to select semantically meaningful moments, or navigating the playhead
for frame-level precision. P2 noted that “the dialogue highlighting
feature was also helpful in finding the right time to add a clip.”
Perceived Effort and Output: Across both studies, participants
strongly agreed that MicroEditor improved their efficiency (median
7.0/7) and enjoyment (median 7.0/7). Editors also felt the assisted
workflow was worth the effort and produced satisfying edits (median 6.0/7 for both). Participants reported better decision support
(median 6.0/7 in both studies) and, in Study 2, higher confidence in
their editing choices (median 6.0/7 vs. 5.0/7 in Study 1). In Study 2,
participants felt the assisted edits improved the video output over
the rough cut (median 6.0/7). P5 noted “the pacing with [MicroEditor] resulted in a much more flowing sequence,” and P7 found “pacing
and flow are better and easier, and save time to iterate and adjust.” An
interactive gallery of video outputs across diverse scenes, including
direct comparisons to the initial rough cuts, is available on our
project page.

Our formative study found that micro-editing involves tedious
frame-by-frame work (F1). With MicroEditor, all nine participants
shifted from low-level timeline manipulation to structured tool
interactions, with fewer total operations needed to achieve their
edits (Table 2). All nine showed a lower operation rate (mean 1.4
vs. 4.9 Ops/min, sign test 𝑝 = 0.004, Cohen’s 𝑑 = −1.57), suggesting that the tools compressed what would otherwise require many
fine-grained manual adjustments into fewer, higher-level actions.
Although participants perceived the tools as reducing effort (P9:
“If I were to manually edit, I would be using fewer cuts for awkward
silences just because of the manual labor that went into each cut”,
P3: “T replaces a lot of clunky movement selecting clips and moving
them”), MicroEditor sessions were not shorter on average (13.1 vs.
11.4 min). Editors appear to have reinvested the reduced mechanical
effort into exploring more alternatives, including an average of 3.6
minutes browsing the reaction shot gallery, a modality that does

not exist in the baseline condition. Because this additional exploration time makes raw duration comparisons between conditions
difficult to interpret, we report operation rates (Ops/min) as the
primary measure of mechanical effort. In the baseline condition,
participants were given freeform editing instructions with no requirement to add reaction shots. Despite this, three participants
(P6, P7, P8) chose to manually create reaction shots by extending
portions of neighboring clips and moving them to the B-roll track.
With MicroEditor, the Reaction Shot Browser replaced this manual
search entirely. Baseline session times therefore likely undercount
the effort needed to search through all available footage for ideal
reaction shots. As P2 reflected, without the tools “it took a lot of
time and I couldn’t easily add additional reaction clips.” Under time
pressure, editors resort to nearby clips instead. P6 noted, “Usually I
would grab a reaction right before and after if I’m in a time crunch.”
Exploration Patterns: Beyond aggregate counts, the interaction
logs reveal how participants used the assisted tools. Following prior
analyses of creative tool usage patterns [24], Figure 8 illustrates
the full operation sequence for each participant. Eight of nine participants began their sessions with rhythm edits (F3), adjusting
pacing by feel before moving to cut offset suggestions (F4) and
reaction shots (F5). This ordering is consistent with our formative
study, where editors described addressing rhythm before visual
attention. P3 was the sole exception, skipping rhythm edits entirely,
likely because the rough cut’s existing pacing already suited their
creative intent. Seven of nine participants also exhibited iterative
refinement at individual cut points, applying a cut offset suggestion,
previewing the result in context, resetting, and trying a different
suggestion before committing. Three participants (P1, P2, P8) compared across rule types at the same cut, for example applying a
cut-on-action suggestion, resetting, then trying a character-focus
suggestion, evaluating how different editorial rationales felt in practice. Usage patterns varied widely. P3 completed the entire session
with only 5 assisted operations (3 cut offset suggestions applied, 2
reaction shots inserted), relying entirely on the system’s defaults.
P8, the most active explorer, touched every cut point, compared
multiple rule types, and inserted 9 reaction shots across 40 total operations. Similar iterative apply-preview-reset patterns have been
observed across other creative domains [45].

7

Discussion

Our evaluations focused on how well MicroEditor supports our
design goals. In this section, we discuss transferable insights from
our design (Section 7.1), how our tools changed the way editors
explored (Section 7.2), and limitations and future work (Section 7.3).

7.1

Discretizing Continuous Creative Spaces

The space of possible micro-edits is continuous: audio and video
cuts can be decoupled by any offset within 𝑊 . It is also multidimensional, since each cut involves competing objectives like rhythm,
character emphasis, and action continuity. Editors reason about
this space through discrete heuristics instead: cut-on-action, cutbefore-blink, favor a character’s screen time. Our core contribution
is turning these heuristics into navigable structures built on audiovisual synchrony windows, which serve two purposes at once. For
the editor, they guarantee perceptual safety: any offset within 𝑊 𝑣
preserves audiovisual flow, so the editor can focus on creative intent
instead of watching for lip-sync errors. For the system, they shrink
the AI’s search space, limiting the frames a VLM must reason about
to a region where every candidate is already valid. Recent work calls
this kind of shared structure a compositional substrate [5]. It makes
both the human’s and the AI’s work interpretable to each other.
Our system goes further by populating these windows with categorized alternatives, so they become a structure for composition,
not a boundary that excludes bad options. The same idea applies to
reaction shots, where low-synchrony regions become candidates
the system indexes by emotion and shot size for browsing (F5).
More broadly, this suggests three design principles for creativity
support in continuous perceptual parameter spaces, such as photo
and video color grading or character animation:
• Formalize domain constraints to compute boundaries within
which any choice is perceptually safe.
• Within those boundaries, surface alternatives categorized by
domain-specific rationale rather than ranked by a single score.
• A single computed constraint can serve more than one purpose:
the same boundary that defines when one operation is safe can
also define candidates for a complementary operation.
We demonstrate these principles in film micro-editing, but they
should apply more broadly. In AI-assisted photo color grading, for
instance, perceptual constraints (skin tones stay natural, highlights
do not clip) could define safe boundaries within which generated
alternatives are grouped by aesthetic intent rather than ranked by
a single quality score. In character animation, the same skeletal rig
that keeps a motion perceptually plausible also lets an animator
reuse it, like walking, across many different characters. Applying
a motion template like this, instead of tuning each joint’s rotation
knobs one by one, turns a continuous space of poses into multiple,
easy-to-navigate discrete choices.

7.2

Lowering the Cost of Exploration

Because any point within 𝑊 is perceptually safe, the system can
surface multiple discrete suggestions at each cut, categorized by
editorial rationale, and populate a reaction shot browser from lowsynchrony regions in the source footage. This categorization is a
deliberate design choice. A single “best” AI output often induces

Nhan (Nathan) Tran, Hijung Valentina Shin, Abe Davis, and Mackenzie Leake

design fixation [53], and when AI auto-fills an entire structure, the
perceived cost of revision stifles exploration [5]. To avoid premature
convergence, our approach explicitly extracts dimensions (editorial
rationales) across which users can explore alternatives, making the
continuous design space legible rather than merely navigable [48].
The interaction logs suggest this strategy worked: participants
compared suggestions drawn from different rationales at the same
cut point before committing (Figure 8), a rapid comparison that
depends on alternatives being computationally and interactively
cheap [19]. By never auto-applying suggestions, our system lets
editors try with one click and revert with another (DG2).
The quantitative logs tell the same story at a broader scale. All
nine logged participants showed a lower operation rate with MicroEditor than in the baseline (Table 2), a shift from frame-level
adjustments to higher-level creative evaluations. Lowering the
cost per attempt did not make sessions shorter. Instead, editors
spent the saved mechanical effort on trying more alternatives, an
instance of what Olsen [30] calls expressive leverage. When a tool
is effective, it does more than speed up standard tasks. It changes
what creators choose to do [44]. P6 explained: “when you’re on
a time crunch, you don’t care that much... you just go with the first
thing you see that works. But here... the cost of trying is a lot lower”
(DG1).
The tedious part of micro-editing is finding viable candidates,
not choosing between them. Dmytryk et al. [14] makes the same
distinction: editors settle for simpler cuts when better ones take
“more time and greater effort,” yet the cut itself depends on a sense
of timing that defines exceptional editors. We see the same pattern here: once search became cheap, editors used techniques they
already knew more often, but still made every timing decision themselves. This points to where automation belongs in creative tools:
the search that comes before a decision. Automating the search
while preserving the choice respects the expertise that editors bring
to every cut.

7.3

Limitations and Future Work

Model Dependencies: Our system relies on off-the-shelf models for synchrony estimation (LR-ASD, AVID-CMA), blink detection (MediaPipe), and a VLM (Gemini 3.0 Flash) for coarse scene
analysis and cut-on-action suggestions via numbered mosaics, and
is bounded by their current capabilities. Current VLMs lack finegrained temporal understanding [37]. Synchrony models can also
struggle with multiple simultaneous speakers or overlapping dialogue, and we assume a single mixed audio track. These models are
pluggable components in our formalization rather than contributions of their own, so improvements to any of them would improve
the system directly, without changing the interaction design.
Scope and Generalization: Our implementation and evaluation
focus on dialogue scenes. Scene types where synchrony plays a
smaller role (voiceovers, music-driven scenes, or action scenes without dialogue) would require new definitions of synchrony and
rhythm, which we leave to future work. The value of micro-editing
also depends on the starting rough cut, and there is no single groundtruth final cut: editing styles varied widely even among our participants (Figure 8, Table 2). Finally, our formalization treats each

micro-edit as a local decision, but editors may want consistency
across cuts, such as cutting consistently early or late on speaker
changes. Conditioning suggestions on an editor’s prior decisions is

Informing Agentic and Generative Video Tools: Current generative video models produce tightly synchronized audio-video
outputs with no mechanism for the audiovisual decoupling that
professional editors rely on to control pacing and attention. Our formalization of the micro-edit space provides a structure for future AI
systems. By applying these synchrony bounds, both agentic editing
tools and video models could produce outputs with explicit rhythm
and view control, bridging micro-editing and macro-editing.

8

Conclusion

While rough cuts establish a film’s narrative, the transition to a
polished fine cut requires navigating a continuous, multidimensional space of micro-edits that has previously remained informal
and manual. Our work formalizes this stage by decoupling audio and video through synchrony windows, transforming a tedious,
frame-by-frame process into a structured computational space. By
surfacing specific micro-edit types, from rhythm control to viewer
attention (cut-on-action, cut-before-blink, and reaction-shot alternatives), we provide a representation that bridges the gap between
raw timeline operations and the editor’s intent. Editors in our studies reported that this structure reduced mechanical effort and allowed them to reinvest their focus into higher-level creative goals.
More broadly, this work demonstrates that computational assistance in creative workflows is most effective when it operates at
this intermediate granularity: providing the structure to navigate
complex decision spaces without automating the creative outcome.

• For earlier cuts (L-cut): Match frames by distance from last
frame, maintaining consistent offset (B−A).
FOCUS ON: Motion trajectory, direction continuity, body
alignment (head angle, shoulder rotation, limb positions), and
temporal alignment.

The temporal alignment rules encode the key insight that frames
are sampled at the same rate (4.5 fps), so valid cut points must match
frames representing the same moment relative to the cut boundary.
Motion Matching: The VLM identifies frame pairs where motion
trajectory, body position, or gaze continues smoothly across the
cut boundary. Each pair (𝑛 out, 𝑛 in ) converts to a timeline offset:
𝛿 = (𝑛 frame − 1)/fpsmosaic

Reaction Shot Indexing

Low-sync segments provide source material for reaction shots. A
segment from 𝑀 𝑗 qualifies as a candidate if:
• 𝜎 (𝑀 𝑗 , 𝑡) = 0 throughout (character visible but not speaking)
• The target character is detected in ≥80% of frames
• The segment is adjacent to spoken material (filtering irrelevant
silent stretches)
We index candidates by character identity and emotion embeddings
(EmoCLIP [16]), enabling the semantic search interface described
in Section 5.4.

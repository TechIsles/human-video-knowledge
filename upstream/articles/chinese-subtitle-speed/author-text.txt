ABSTRACT
Subtitled audiovisual content is being consumed more than ever,
and the increase in subtitle speeds has generated debates about
what counts as a comfortable speed for viewers. In China,
subtitling has a long tradition with a standard speed of about 5
characters per second (cps), whereas international platforms such
as Netflix adopt a faster rate of 9 cps. Yet, little is known about
how such rapid speeds affect the viewing experience. This study
examines the impact of subtitle speed on Chinese viewers by
testing 31 participants who watched English-language videos
subtitled into Chinese at both 5 and 9 cps. Using a mixed-
methods design, we assessed comprehension, recall, cognitive
load, enjoyment, satisfaction, reading behaviour, perceptions and
preferences. Results show that whereas faster subtitles did not
negatively affect comprehension, they resulted in impaired recall,
higher cognitive load, lower enjoyment and worse satisfaction.
Eye-tracking further revealed shorter dwell time and fewer revisits
at 9 cps, limiting viewers’ ability to finish reading or re-reading
subtitles and possibly reducing processing depth. These findings
highlight that Chinese viewers benefited from the slower display
time of 5 cps subtitles and provide empirical evidence to inform
future subtitling standards and practices, with implications for
enhancing the viewing experience.

1. Introduction
Subtitled video content is consumed worldwide, a trend fuelled by the rise of streaming
platforms such as Amazon Prime Video, Disney+ and Netflix. As demand for subtitles in
digital media grows, the industry has shifted toward higher subtitle speeds (Díaz-Cintas
& Remael, 2021). The pervasiveness of subtitling, combined with the transition from
standard to increasingly faster subtitle speeds, presents a timely opportunity to investi­
gate how speed affects viewers’ processing of subtitled videos.
    To date, most studies on the impact of subtitle speed on viewers’ processing of sub­
titled videos have been conducted in Western language contexts, such as English,


Polish, Dutch, and Spanish, with research dating back to the 1980s and continuing to the
present (d’Ydewalle et al., 1985, 1987; Jensema, 1998; Koolstra et al., 1999; Kruger et al.,
2022; Liao et al., 2021; Muylaert et al., 1983; Szarkowska et al., 2016, 2024, 2025; Szar­
kowska & Bogucka, 2019; Szarkowska & Gerber-Morón, 2018). Although China has a
long and rich history of subtitling practices (Jia & Díaz-Cintas, 2026; Jin & Jia, 2025),
experimental and cognitive approaches to subtitling processing research remain rela­
tively underrepresented (Díaz-Cintas & Szarkowska, 2020; Jia, 2023), even if they have
begun to expand in recent years (Cui et al., 2023; Cui & Liu, 2024; Jiang & Doherty,
2024; Liao et al., 2020; Wang & Pellicer-Sánchez, 2023). The increasing focus on recep­
tion studies centred on viewers’ perceptions, preferences and experiences has contributed
to the growing maturity of audiovisual translation (AVT) research (Chaume, 2018; Di
Giovanni & Gambier, 2018; Valdeón, 2022). However, to the best of our knowledge,
the topic of subtitle speed has not yet been systematically examined in the Chinese
context. Given China’s population of over 1.4 billion people (National Bureau of Stat­
istics, 2026) and its vast audience for audiovisual media, there is a clear need to
extend reception-oriented subtitle speed research to non-Western contexts and non-
alphabetic script languages, such as Chinese. Broadening this scope would not only
improve the viewing experience for Chinese audiences but also enrich the global research
landscape and help validate and generalise existing scientific findings in subtitling
research.
    The present study addresses this gap by adopting a mixed-methods eye-tracking
approach to examine the processing and reception of video with interlingual Chinese1
subtitles in terms of viewers’ comprehension, recall, cognitive load, enjoyment, satisfac­
tion and reading behaviour under two subtitle speed conditions: a speed considered stan­
dard in China (5 characters per second, cps) and a faster speed commonly used by Netflix
(9 cps).


2. Subtitle speed
Interlingual subtitling goes beyond translating original dialogue exchanges as it requires
linguistic condensation and reformulation, which is largely governed by the speed at
which subtitles appear on screen, commonly referred to as ‘subtitle speed’, but also
known as ‘subtitle display rate’, ‘presentation rate’ or ‘reading speed’ (Díaz-Cintas &
Remael, 2021; Kruger et al., 2022). More specifically, this concept refers to the time
viewers need to read and assimilate the information contained in a subtitle while simul­
taneously following the onscreen action and listening to the dialogue. Its calculation is
based on the duration the subtitle remains on screen and the number of characters,
including blank spaces and punctuation, that make up a given subtitle. This speed can
be measured in words per minute (wpm) or, increasingly in the AVT industry, in char­
acters per second (cps) (Szarkowska & Bogucka, 2019).
   Subtitle speeds vary not only across countries, but also among broadcasters, pro­
duction companies and language service providers. Many countries, such as Denmark
(FBO, 2019), the Netherlands (Auteursbond, 2022) and the UK (Ofcom, 2017), have
official technical subtitling guidelines for their respective languages. These typically
specify maximum subtitle speeds ranging from 10 to 20 cps, depending on the target
audience, such as hearing viewers, d/Deaf or hard-of-hearing viewers, and different


age groups. In practice, however, subtitle speed is not fixed, but varies with content pace,
speech rates and the need to follow specific editing conventions (Fresno & Sepielak,
2022).
    Subtitle speed cannot be considered in isolation from the linguistic properties of the
target language and the cognitive processes involved in viewers’ perception. As a logo­
graphic writing system (Ma et al., 2020), Chinese poses unique cognitive demands that
differ from those associated with alphabetic languages, making its characters not directly
comparable to those used in alphabetic languages such as English. Unlike a single English
letter, each Chinese character carries dense semantic and visual information (Kao et al.,
2002). The complexity of reading in Chinese is compounded by the lack of spaces
between characters, which requires active segmentation and compels viewers to rely
heavily on linguistic and contextual cues to extract meaning (Cui & Liu, 2024; Zhu
et al., 2019). This, in turn, may increase cognitive load and result in slower reading
speeds compared to alphabetic systems (Bai et al., 2008).
    With respect to reading behaviour, the visual and structural complexity of Chinese
characters has been argued to influence eye-movement patterns, potentially leading to
longer fixations, shorter saccades and smaller perceptual spans (Liversedge et al., 2016;
Sun et al., 1985). However, evidence from eye-tracking studies comparing Chinese and
alphabetic scripts remains limited and does not permit strong generalisations (Feng,
2009; Liversedge et al., 2016; Sun et al., 1985). While these studies suggest that
Chinese readers may process fewer characters per fixation than readers of alphabetic
scripts, Liversedge et al. (2016) emphasise the small number of available studies and
their methodological constraints, and note that reliable cross-language differences
were not consistently observed in earlier work (Feng, 2009; Sun et al., 1985).
    The aforementioned language-specific constraints directly affect subtitle speed and its
regulation in subtitling guidelines. While the standards for alphabetic languages, such as
English as the lingua franca, commonly allow up to 20 cps (Netflix, n.d.), such thresholds
cannot be directly applied to non-alphabetic languages such as Chinese. In academic dis­
course, 5 cps has traditionally been considered standard in Chinese subtitling (Kuo,
2017), and some institutions, such as the China International Communication Centre
(2025, personal communication), still prescribe this limit officially. However, inter­
national streaming platforms have begun adopting faster rates in response to changing
viewing habits and growing audiovisual output. Netflix, for example, now permits up
to 9 cps for Chinese subtitles (Netflix, 2022), reflecting a shift towards faster display
rates that mirror those seen in alphabetic-language subtitling.
    In the context of Chinese streaming platforms, subtitle speed is rarely specified expli­
citly in characters per second. Instead, major platforms such as iQIYI, Tencent Video and
Youku typically regulate subtitles indirectly, most often by limiting the maximum
number of characters per line (usually 14–16 Chinese characters), without clarifying
whether blank spaces used instead of punctuation count toward that character limit (per­
sonal communication with industry professionals, 2025).2 This lack of explicit speed-
related parameters is mirrored in academic research, where subtitle speed in Chinese
contexts remains underexplored and insufficiently supported by evidence (Ma & Xie,
2019; Qian, 2004; Wang, 2015), unlike in Western settings, where it has been widely
studied using experimental and cognitive approaches, as discussed in the next section.


3. Previous experimental eye-tracking research on subtitle speed
Research into subtitle speed began in Belgium in the 1980s with foundational studies by
Muylaert et al. (1983) and d’Ydewalle et al. (1985, 1987), involving Dutch-speaking par­
ticipants. Although early experiments used imprecise descriptors such as ‘normal speed’
or ‘longer/shorter than usual’, d’Ydewalle et al. (1987) tested subtitles displayed with or
without sound at three different speeds and found the optimum to be the so-called six-
second rule (equivalent of 10.5 cps, with max. 32 characters per line). Their study is con­
sidered to have provided evidence supporting this rule, which was prevalent in the sub­
titling industry at that time.
    More recent work has used eye tracking to explore subtitle speed and text editing, as
less condensation results in higher speeds. Szarkowska et al. (2016) examined how
editing (edited vs. near-verbatim) and speed (12 vs. 15 cps) affected comprehension
and reading behaviour in Polish viewers watching interlingual and intralingual Polish
subtitles. Reducing speed to 12 cps through editing did not improve comprehension, par­
ticularly for intralingual subtitles. Eye-movements data supported this pattern: the
slower, edited subtitles prompted more gaze shifts between image and text and propor­
tionally less time spent reading the subtitle area, suggesting that higher subtitle speeds
paired with unedited text may facilitate smoother processing.
    Szarkowska and Bogucka (2019) re-evaluated the six-second rule with slow- and fast-
speech rate English-language videos with Polish interlingual subtitles, requiring a low
and high degree of text condensation respectively. They found that maintaining the
slow speed (12 cps) during fast-paced dialogue necessitated heavy condensation and sig­
nificantly lowered comprehension. They concluded that young contemporary audiences
with higher English proficiency might benefit from subtitle speeds faster than 12 cps,
because they allow for more complete, less condensed text.
    Szarkowska and Gerber-Morón (2018) tested the effects of subtitle speed on compre­
hension, cognitive load, enjoyment and eye movements of English, Polish and Spanish
speakers, watching inter- and intralingual subtitles with English and Hungarian audio
at speeds ranging from 12 to 20 cps. Slow subtitles resulted in more re-reading,
causing more frustration and less enjoyment. Participants preferred faster subtitles
with unreduced text for English-language videos, but slower subtitles with condensed
text for Hungarian videos, a language unfamiliar to them.
    A limitation of the above studies is that they conflated speed with text condensation
and editing, introducing confounds, as shortening subtitles to reduce speed may
influence processing independently of speed. To avoid this, the current study, like Liao
et al. (2021) and its replication by Szarkowska et al. (2024), varied subtitle speed
without condensing the text. Liao et al. (2021) manipulated subtitle speed in English sub­
titles (12, 20 and 28 cps), but, to avoid subtitles becoming unsynchronised with the
English narration, they removed the audio track (including dialogue, music and sound
effects). Their experiment, conducted with English-speaking students from Macquarie
University in Australia, showed that very fast subtitles (28 cps) impaired comprehension
and led to fewer and shorter fixations, as well as fewer gaze shifts between subtitles and
video. Word-level analyses showed that established effects from reading research, such as
word frequency and word length, whereby longer, less frequent words received more
visual attention at slower speeds, were attenuated at higher speeds.


   Analysing the same dataset, Kruger et al. (2022) examined word skipping and reread­
ing as indicators of processing depth. They found that faster speeds increased word skip­
ping and decreased rereading, suggesting a negative impact on both lexical access and
higher-level comprehension.
   Szarkowska et al. (2024) replicated the study by Liao et al. (2021) with British and
Polish participants in the UK and Poland. The results confirmed that subtitle speed sig­
nificantly influenced reading behaviour in both groups. As in the original study, 28-cps
subtitles were often too fast, causing participants to miss final words. These findings
support the robustness of subtitle speed effects and extend them to both native and
non-native English speakers.
   More recently, Szarkowska et al. (2025) have investigated the effects of subtitle speed
and individual differences on subtitle processing in both native and non-native viewers
using intralingual English subtitles. The study demonstrates that non-native viewers were
more strongly affected by faster subtitle speeds than native viewers, and that individual
differences such as language proficiency and working memory capacity significantly
modulated processing behaviour.
   While the studies discussed above offer valuable insights into subtitle processing, they
focus on Western contexts and alphabetic languages. Research on non-alphabetic writing
systems such as Chinese remains limited. Given the linguistic and cognitive differences in
reading logographic scripts, further investigation is needed to determine whether
findings from alphabetic-language contexts generalise to Chinese audiences.


4. Current study
This study aims to investigate how Chinese viewers perceive and process English-
language videos subtitled in Chinese at different subtitle speeds.
   We addressed two research questions:
  RQ1: How does subtitle speed affect viewers’ comprehension, recall, cognitive load, enjoy­
  ment and satisfaction?

  RQ2: How does subtitle speed affect viewers’ reading behaviour?

Regarding RQ1, we hypothesised that faster subtitle speeds may negatively impact com­
prehension, recall, enjoyment and satisfaction, while increasing cognitive load. These
heightened demands may manifest as greater perceived difficulty, increased effort and
higher frustration.
   As for RQ2, we predicted that fast subtitle speeds would lead to reduced time spent in
the subtitle area, as evidenced by dwell time and fewer revisits, and to more shallow pro­
cessing, as evidenced by shorter mean fixation duration. This is likely due to the shorter
display time of fast subtitles, which may force viewers to read the subtitles quickly while
simultaneously trying to follow the visual content on screen.


5. Method
Using a mixed-methods approach (Creswell & Creswell, 2018), we collected quantitative
data through self-reported questionnaires on cognitive load, enjoyment and satisfaction,


as well as comprehension and recall post-tests and eye-tracking measures. Qualitative
insights came from semi-structured interviews. Following Creswell and Creswell’s
(2018) convergent mixed-methods design, we integrated both strands to examine
whether the findings converged or diverged. [Direct quotation from another publication omitted.]
   To the best of our knowledge, this is the first experimental study in the Chinese
context to investigate comprehensively how different subtitle speeds affect viewers’
viewing experience, using a mixed-methods design combining eye tracking, self-reported
questionnaires and semi-structured interviews. This approach offers insights into how
viewers simultaneously process subtitles, visuals and audio in a dynamic, multimodal
context.


5.1. Participants
The study involved 31 native Chinese-speaking participants (Female = 16, Male = 15;
Mage = 23.48, SD = 3.58, range = 20–34). Their education levels included 12 (39%) with
undergraduate degrees, 17 (55%) with postgraduate degrees, and two (6%) with doctoral
degrees. All had normal or corrected-to-normal vision and no dyslexia. Before viewing
English-language clips with subtitles in Chinese, their English proficiency was assessed
using the Cambridge General English Proficiency Test,3 with scores ranging from 16
to 24 out of 25 (M = 18.55, SD = 1.95), corresponding to the Common European Frame­
work of Reference for Languages (CEFR) levels B2 to C2. To gauge familiarity with the
task of reading subtitles, participants rated how often they watched English programmes
with Chinese subtitles on a scale from 1 (never) to 5 (always). The mean was 3.35 (SD =
1.05, range = 2–5), suggesting the activity was familiar. Regarding AVT preference, 29
(94%) participants chose subtitling, one (3%) participant preferred dubbing, and one
(3%) participant chose the original version. None selected ‘I never watch foreign audio­
visual productions’. Additionally, 30 (97%) subjects had no prior exposure to the stimuli
(Section 5.2), minimising the influence of familiarity. The study was approved by the
Research Ethics Committee of the lead author’s institution, and all participants provided
informed consent.


5.2. Stimuli
Participants viewed two clips from the Netflix series The Chair (Amanda Peet and Annie
Julia Wyman, 2021), a comedy-drama about a newly appointed head of an English
department at a struggling American university who tries to save her department
while navigating institutional bureaucracy, generational clashes and fallout from a col­
league’s scandal. The selected material consisted of several short excerpts from a single
narrative thread, as the series interweaves multiple storylines. Clip 1 was taken from
Season 1, Episode 1, lasted 4 min and 33 s, and contained 70 subtitles. Clip 2 was
taken from Season 1, Episode 2, lasted 4 min and 27 s, and contained 72 subtitles. The
clips were self-contained, required no prior plot knowledge, and featured fast-paced, dia­
logue-rich scenes with two to four characters. Using clips from the same series helped
reduce variability in audiovisual style and production features.


   Two Chinese subtitle sets were created for each clip, representing different subtitle
speeds: 5 and 9 cps. The two versions differed only in the timing of subtitles, whereas
the text was the same in the two conditions. The minimum subtitle duration was set
to 1 s to avoid ‘ghost titles’ (Kruger & Liao, 2025), and the maximum duration was
limited to 6 s. The clips were presented with the original English audio to maintain eco­
logical validity (Mellinger & Hanson, 2022).
   Participants watched both clips: one with the standard speed (5 cps) and the other the
faster speed (9 cps). Speed conditions were counterbalanced, and clip presentation was
randomised to control for order and clip effects.


5.3. Apparatus
An SMI RED eye tracker with a 250-Hz sampling rate was used to record participants’ eye
movements. SMI software package Experiment Centre was utilised to create and conduct
the experiment. BeGaze 3.7.40 was employed to draw individual areas of interest (AOI)
around each subtitle and pre-process the results. Eye-tracking data quality was assessed
using tracking ratio. All participants demonstrated a tracking ratio above 80%, and no
participants were excluded from further analysis (Holmqvist et al., 2011).


5.4. Procedure
Participants were tested individually in a research laboratory. Before the experiment, they
were informed that they would take part in a study on the quality of subtitles. On arrival,
participants signed informed consent forms and took the Cambridge English Proficiency
Test. They were then seated in a stable chair 60 cm from a 22-inch LCD monitor (1920 ×
1200 resolution) and engaged in an experiment that lasted approximately one hour. The
procedure began with participants filling out a demographic questionnaire displayed on a
screen, recording details such as age, gender, English proficiency, educational back­
ground and previous experiences and preferences related to subtitling. Before the
main viewing task, a 5-point calibration procedure was performed. Calibration accuracy
was accepted only if the average deviation did not exceed 1 degree of visual angle
(Holmqvist et al., 2011). Following calibration, eye movements were tracked as partici­
pants viewed an English-spoken video subtitled in Chinese. After viewing each clip,
they responded to an onscreen questionnaire about the video content and their
viewing experience. Following the viewings and completion of the questionnaires, par­
ticipants were interviewed in Chinese, where they expressed their perceptions and pre­
ferences regarding subtitle speeds, the influence of subtitle speeds on the viewing
experience and their general views on the quality of subtitling. The session concluded
with a debriefing.


5.5. Design
The experiment employed a within-subjects design, with subtitle speed as the indepen­
dent variable, set at two levels: 5 and 9 cps. The dependent variables included compre­
hension, recall, cognitive load, enjoyment, satisfaction and eye-tracking measures.


5.5.1. Comprehension
Comprehension was assessed through ten true/false questions about the clip’s general
content, calculated as a percentage of correct responses. [Stimulus-derived example omitted.]


5.5.2. Recall
Recall was measured as the percentage of correct responses to ten multiple-choice ques­
tions following the viewing of each clip. Participants were asked to recognise the exact
phrasing from the subtitles in multiple-choice questions with three options: one
correct answer, two distractors, and ‘I don’t know’. The phrasing generally differed at
the end of the subtitle, based on the assumption that if a subtitle is too fast, the viewer
may not have time to read it to the end. [Stimulus-derived example and answer choices omitted.]


5.5.3. Cognitive load
Cognitive load (Sweller et al., 2011) was assessed using three dimensions adapted from
the NASA-TLX instrument: difficulty, effort and frustration (Hart & Staveland, 1988;
Szarkowska & Gerber-Morón, 2018).  [Questionnaire wording omitted.] Measurement was on a 7-point scale (1 = very low, 7 = very high).


5.5.4. Enjoyment
Enjoyment was measured with the first item from the Intrinsic Motivation Inventory
(Ryan, 1982). [Questionnaire item wording omitted.] Measured on a 7-point scale (1 = not at all, 7 = very much).


5.5.5. Satisfaction
Participants were invited to evaluate their satisfaction with different subtitle speeds using
a 7-point scale (1 = very dissatisfied, 7 = very satisfied) [Questionnaire item wording omitted].

6. Analyses
We analysed the data as follows. To answer RQ1 (‘How does subtitle speed affect viewers’
comprehension, recall, cognitive load, enjoyment and satisfaction?’), we conducted
paired-samples t tests comparing the standard speed (5 cps) to the fast speed (9 cps).
Seven t tests were run in total. Four t tests were conducted for comprehension, recall,
enjoyment and satisfaction (one for each); three tests were run to assess cognitive load
(difficulty, effort, frustration). Normality was assessed using z scores for skewness and
kurtosis (Field, 2024; Silva et al., 2022) and confirmed via histograms and boxplots.
All data met the assumption (z scores < 1.96), except for recall (z scores = 2.75 and
4.03). Instead of using the non-parametric Wilcoxon signed-rank test, which could
reduce the power of the analysis, we validated our results using a 95% bias-corrected
and accelerated (BCa) bootstrap with 5,000 re-samples. Bootstrapping does not make
assumptions about the shape of the data, and therefore does not rely on normality
(Fox & Weisberg, 2018). The technique creates a pre-determined number of data
samples (here, 5,000 samples) by resampling the original data (with replacement). The
analysis is then run on the 5,000 samples, and the average result of these samples is com­
pared against the original sample. In bootstrapping, particularly in BCa bootstrapping, a
correction is applied to control for skewness or other bias in the data. BCa is particularly
useful for small sample sizes (Deng et al., 2013; Farag et al., 2025), as is the case in this
paper. The bootstrapped results confirmed the validity of our results.
    To answer RQ2 (‘How does subtitle speed affect viewers’ reading behaviour?’), we con­
ducted three generalised linear mixed models (GLMMs), one per dependent variable:
dwell time (DT), mean fixation duration (MFD) and number of revisits. In line with
the temporal cut-off method, data points in DT and MFD below 60 ms as well as
MFD above 800 ms were excluded from the analysis (Eskenazi, 2024). DT was mildly
skewed and was square-root transformed; MFD was severely skewed, so it was log trans­
formed. Revisits, a count variable, was analysed using a Poisson distribution with a log
link function (Heck et al., 2012). All data met the assumptions of normality, homogeneity
of variance, and linearity of residuals, and there were no signs of collinearity or outliers.
    All quantitative analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS version 30. The alpha level
was set at 0.05. For the analyses involving the three eye-tracking measures, a Bonferroni
correction was applied to control for multiple comparisons, resulting in an adjusted
alpha level of 0.016 (Von Der Malsburg & Angele, 2017). See Appendix 1 for details
on how the mixed models were built, for all fixed and random effects used during mod­
elling, and for the final models.


  In addition, a qualitative analysis of data from semi-structured interviews was con­
ducted to gain deeper insight into their viewing perceptions, preferences and experiences,
and to complement the interpretation of the quantitative findings.

7.6. Eye-tracking measures
RQ2 investigated the impact of subtitle speed on reading behaviour. As can be seen from
descriptive statistics in Table 7 and Figure 1 below, slower subtitles had higher DT and
higher number of revisits, but similar MFD to faster subtitles. The GLMMs confirmed
these results (see Appendix 1 for the full models).
   Because slow subtitles remained on screen for longer, viewers took advantage of the
additional display time and spent significantly more time in the subtitle area when watch­
ing subtitles presented at 5 cps than at the faster speed of 9 cps (p < 0.001), as reflected in
DT. As shown in Appendix 1, proficiency in English had no significant impact on the
time spent in the subtitles (p = 0.269); there was also no interaction between proficiency
and speed (p = 0.711). Similarly, familiarity with subtitling did not significantly impact
the time spent by viewers in the subtitle area (p = 0.316), irrespective of subtitle speed.
   Subtitle speed did not affect the depth of processing, as measured by MFD (p = 0.698).
English proficiency likewise had no impact on MFD (p = 0.427), and there was no inter­
action between speed and proficiency (p = 0.692), irrespective of subtitle speed.

8. Discussion
This study aimed to explore how subtitle speeds affect Chinese viewers’ experience when
watching English videos with Chinese subtitles. We posed two research questions, which
are addressed below.


8.1. RQ1: how does subtitle speed affect viewers’ comprehension, recall,
cognitive load, enjoyment and satisfaction?
The faster subtitle speed did not negatively affect viewers’ comprehension. This finding
aligns with earlier studies by Szarkowska et al. (2016) and Szarkowska and Gerber-Morón
(2018), which also reported that increased subtitle speed did not impair comprehension.
Similarly, Liao et al. (2021) and its replication by Szarkowska et al. (2024), found no sig­
nificant difference in comprehension between 12 and 20 cps. In Liao et al. (2021), and
Kruger et al. (2022), reporting on the same experiment as Liao et al. (2021), a difference
emerged only when 12 and 28 cps were compared, but this was not replicated by Szar­
kowska et al. (2024), who found no differences in comprehension across any of the
tested speeds. Taken together, these findings suggest that faster subtitle speeds do not
necessarily compromise comprehension, at least up to 20 cps.
   One possible explanation for why the faster subtitle speed did not negatively affect
comprehension is that the questionnaire items in Liao et al. (2021) combined compre­
hension and mnemonic elements, corresponding to what Szarkowska et al. (2025)
termed ‘Recall’, and often required participants to remember specific details, numbers
or exact wording from subtitles. This conflation may have introduced confounds,
making it more difficult to isolate the effect of subtitle speed on comprehension rather
than recall. Another possible explanation is participants’ familiarity with subtitled
media. In the present study, as in Szarkowska et al. (2024, 2025), participants generally
reported being familiar with watching English-language programmes with Chinese


subtitles. By contrast, in Liao et al. (2021), as reported in Kruger et al. (2022), familiarity
with subtitling was relatively low (2.78 on a 1–7 scale, where 1 indicated low and 7 high
familiarity). Greater familiarity with subtitling may therefore help viewers cope better
with higher subtitle speeds. This interpretation is consistent with evidence that subtitling
remains a dominant and preferred mode of audiovisual translation among Chinese
viewers (Li, 2024).
    Unlike general comprehension, which was unaffected by speed, recall declined signifi­
cantly at the higher speed – a result also found for English native speakers by Szarkowska
et al. (2025). This suggests that faster subtitles may hinder viewers’ ability to fully read,
process and remember the content, particularly specific textual details appearing towards
the end of the subtitle. One possible explanation is that limited time does not allow
viewers to finish reading or engage in deep cognitive processing. This contrasts with Szar­
kowska and Gerber-Morón (2018), who found that slower subtitles (12 cps) did not
improve recall accuracy (which they called ‘subtitle recognition’) compared to faster sub­
titles (20 cps). A likely reason for this difference is that, as discussed in Section 2, Chinese
subtitles may be processed more slowly than subtitles in alphabetic languages, due to the
visual complexity of Chinese characters and the absence of inter-word spacing (Bai et al.,
2008; Zhu et al., 2019), although this explanation still requires further empirical evidence.
Functionally, 9 cps in Chinese may therefore be more comparable to 28 cps in Liao et al.
(2021) and Kruger et al. (2022).
    Regarding cognitive load, the faster subtitles were perceived as significantly more
demanding, that is, more difficult, effortful and frustrating. As many interviewees
explained, fast subtitles often disappeared before they could be fully read, forcing
viewers to rely more on the English audio. However, this added to their cognitive
burden, as English was not their native language. While Szarkowska and Gerber-
Morón (2018) found no significant effect of subtitle speed on perceived difficulty or
effort, and even reported greater frustration with slower subtitles (12 cps), our findings
suggest this pattern may not apply to Chinese viewers. A likely explanation is the ortho­
graphic complexity of Chinese characters, which may require longer processing time
than alphabetic scripts (Bai et al., 2008; Zhu et al., 2019). In contrast, the standard
slower subtitles used in this study (5 cps) were not perceived as staying on screen too
long. Instead, many participants appreciated them for allowing enough time to read or
reread the content, contributing to a more comfortable viewing experience.
    Viewers reported significantly greater enjoyment when watching videos with stan­
dard, slower subtitles. This contrasts with findings by Szarkowska and Gerber-Morón
(2018), who observed higher enjoyment levels with faster subtitles. As already discussed,
one possible explanation lies in the visual complexity of Chinese characters, which far
exceeds that of Latin alphabets. As a result, Chinese viewers may require more time
and cognitive effort to process the subtitles (Liversedge et al., 2016; Sun et al., 1985).
Faster subtitle speeds in Chinese can, therefore, lead to difficulties in reading and proces­
sing, causing frustration and reducing enjoyment. As many participants noted in inter­
views, faster subtitles led to more skipping, making viewers miss some text and indicating
that they struggled to keep up. This increased sense of effort detracted from their viewing
experience.
    Subtitle speed significantly influenced satisfaction, with slower, standard subtitles con­
tributing to a more satisfying viewing experience. This aligns with the opinions expressed


by participants during the interviews. [Direct participant quotations omitted.]


8.2. RQ2: how does subtitle speed affect viewers’ reading behaviour?
Subtitle speed significantly affected time spent in the subtitle area, with slow subtitles
triggering more reading time in absolute terms than fast subtitles. Because slow subtitles
contained the same text but remained on screen for longer, they gave viewers more time
to read and encouraged more thorough processing, rather than indicating greater proces­
sing difficulty. The dwell time results suggest that participants may have been better able
to integrate information from the subtitles, spoken dialogue and visuals (Kruger et al.,
2022). This finding is consistent with prior research reporting statistically significant
differences in dwell time, referred to as ‘absolute reading time’ in Koolstra et al. (1999)
and in Szarkowska and Gerber-Morón (2018), and as ‘total time’ in Szarkowska et al.
(2025). It also aligns with participants’ reports that slower subtitles were easier to cope
with, as the lack of time pressure made reading feel more relaxed. This pattern is also
plausible from the perspective of Chinese reading research. Chinese reading appears to
involve a relatively narrow perceptual span, estimated at around one character to the
left and three to four characters to the right of fixation, while readers must also
segment a continuous, unspaced string of characters into words online (Inhoff & Liu,
1998; Li & Pollatsek, 2020). In a subtitled multimodal environment, these constraints
make display time particularly important.
    Mean fixation duration, a potential indicator of cognitive load and processing depth
(Holmqvist et al., 2011), was not affected by subtitle speed in the present study. This con­
trasts with some earlier subtitling research showing shorter MFD for faster subtitles,
interpreted as evidence of more superficial processing (Liao et al., 2021). This null
effect should nevertheless be interpreted cautiously. Fixation durations in subtitle
reading should not be expected to mirror those in printed-text reading. In silent
English reading of printed text, mean fixation durations are typically around 200–
250 ms (Rayner, 1998), yet eye-tracking research has shown that subtitle reading may
yield shorter fixation durations (Liao et al., 2021; Szarkowska & Bogucka, 2019; Wang
& Pellicer-Sánchez, 2023). Against this background, the relatively short MFD observed
here (approximately 184–187 ms) is consistent with subtitle reading as a compressed,
externally paced mode of processing and lower than the fixation durations typically
reported for printed Chinese (245 ms in Liversedge et al., 2016). Under such conditions,
increases in subtitle speed may be more likely to affect broader temporal measures, such
as dwell time and revisits, than per-fixation duration. Similarly, and in line with Szar­
kowska and Bogucka (2019), MFD was not affected by English proficiency. This lack
of significance may partly reflect substantial interindividual variability in MFD (Holmq­
vist et al., 2011), but may also indicate that, in the present Chinese subtitling context,
MFD was less sensitive to processing difficulty than dwell time and revisits.
    Revisits may reflect viewers’ engagement with linguistic processing, i.e. making sense
of the text (Kruger et al., 2022). The significant difference in the number of revisits


between slow and fast subtitle speeds indicates that viewers re-read subtitles more often
at the slower speed. This likely resulted from the longer display time, which may have
allowed more thorough processing and the resolution of linguistic ambiguities. In con­
trast, faster subtitles may not have left enough time for viewers to return to and reprocess
the text, limiting opportunities for clarification and reinforcement of understanding.
    This pattern matches Kruger et al. (2022), who highlight the importance of re-reading
in multimodal viewing contexts. In such settings, re-reading allows viewers to correct
oculomotor errors or comprehension lapses during linguistic processing and to integrate
verbal information with visual elements to construct a coherent situation model of the
video. When subtitles disappear too quickly, these strategies may be constrained,
leading to fewer revisits or more skipping (Kruger et al., 2022). Szarkowska and
Gerber-Morón (2018) also reported more revisits with slower subtitles, but cautioned
that leaving subtitles on screen too long may disrupt reading flow, as viewers might
return to already processed text.
    The revisit effect may be especially meaningful in Chinese, where written text does not
normally mark word boundaries with spaces. Readers must therefore segment continu­
ous character strings into words, and eye-movement studies show that segmentation
ambiguity increases processing cost and relies on contextual information for resolution
(Bai et al., 2008; Li & Pollatsek, 2020; Zang et al., 2013). Revisits may thus support not
only monitoring and rereading but also re-segmentation, clarification and integration
when initial processing is incomplete (Huang & Li, 2020). Supporting evidence comes
from Cui and Liu’s (2024) eye-tracking study on Chinese subtitles, where inserting
spaces between semantically coherent subtitle segments reduced processing effort and
significantly decreased regressive saccades. This suggests that rereading in Chinese sub­
titling may be partly linked to the effort required to segment and disambiguate unspaced
character strings. The sharp reduction in revisits at 9 cps may therefore indicate not only
less time to look back, but also a constraint on a useful repair-and-integration
mechanism.
    Like many experimental studies, this research inevitably has certain limitations. The
sample comprised young, educated Chinese viewers with relatively high English profi­
ciency and normal hearing, excluding groups such as children, older adults, individuals
with lower education levels or limited English proficiency, or d/Deaf and hard-of-hearing
viewers, who may face greater challenges with faster subtitles. While the sample rep­
resents an important consumer segment of streaming platforms and is therefore appro­
priate for this study, it cannot fully capture the diversity of Chinese audiences. The study
was also limited to fiction; future research could examine whether other genres produce
different effects. In addition, the relatively short clips limit insights into longer-term
effects such as fatigue. These limitations point to the need for more nuanced investi­
gations of subtitling reception in the Chinese context and beyond.


9. Conclusion
This study has investigated subtitle speed, a topic that remains under-researched in
China, and, through a mixed-methods approach, examined how different subtitle
speeds affect the viewing experience of Chinese audiences with interlingual Chinese sub­
titles. Our results suggest that, although the faster speed of 9 cps did not impact


comprehension, it negatively affected all other measures: recall, cognitive load, enjoy­
ment and satisfaction. It also reduced reading time, limiting viewers’ ability to finish
reading or to re-read subtitles, and thereby possibly reducing the depth of processing.
Overall, participants appear to benefit from the slower display time of subtitles of 5
cps. These findings may help improve subtitling standards and practices, enhance
viewers’ experience, and inform the training of future subtitlers.
